The Macquarie Point Stadium Debate

A simplified guide to Hobart's new stadium plan: the arguments, and what is happening now

1. Overview

The Macquarie Point Stadium is a new stadium being planned for Hobart, the capital city of Tasmania, Australia. It will cost about $1.13 billion and will have a roof and seats for around 23,000 people. It is being built on land near the city centre, next to the water.

The main reason for building it is that the Australian Football League (AFL) — the country's biggest football competition — said that Tasmania could only get its own AFL team, the Tasmania Devils, if the state built this new stadium. The Devils will join the AFL in 2028.

This project has caused a lot of disagreement in Tasmania. People argue about the cost, the location, the effect on local history and culture, and whether the money should be spent on other things instead, like hospitals or housing.

The Tasmanian parliament officially approved the project in December 2025. But the argument about it has not stopped — people are still discussing the cost and how it is being managed, even though building has already started.

2. How the Story Began

Tasmania has wanted its own AFL team since the late 1980s, but for many years the league said no. Things changed when the AFL finally agreed — but only if a new stadium was built first.

  • 1987–2023Tasmania tries eight times to get an AFL team. Every attempt fails.
  • 2 May 2023The AFL finally agrees to give Tasmania a team. But there is a condition: Tasmania must build a new roofed stadium with at least 23,000 seats at Macquarie Point.
  • 2024The stadium's estimated cost rises from about $715 million to $945 million.
  • 17 Sept 2025An independent group called the Tasmanian Planning Commission publishes a report. It says the stadium should not be built, because it is "too big" for the site. On the same day, the government announces the cost has risen again — to $1.13 billion.
  • 13 Nov 2025The lower house of parliament votes 25 to 9 in favour of the stadium.
  • 23 Nov 2025Thousands of people join a protest called "Our Place — No New Stadium" outside parliament in Hobart.
  • 3–4 Dec 2025After a very long debate — including a three-hour speech against the stadium by politician Meg Webb — the upper house of parliament votes 9 to 5 in favour. This is the final approval needed.
  • Mar 2026The Australian federal government agrees to give $240 million toward redeveloping the wider area around the stadium (not the stadium itself).
  • Apr 2026A master plan for the whole area is published. Two construction companies are shortlisted to build the stadium.
  • Jul–Aug 2026Early construction work begins at the site. A request from the Greens political party to make the government reveal an updated cost is rejected in parliament, 25 votes to 7.

3. Arguments For the Stadium
Arguments in favour

It is required for AFL entry

Supporters say Tasmania has no real choice. The AFL has clearly said that without the stadium, the Devils could lose their place in the competition. Politicians like Premier Jeremy Rockliff argue that giving up on the stadium now would mean giving up on the whole AFL dream.

A long-awaited dream for Tasmanians

Many Tasmanians have wanted an AFL team for decades. Supporters say the stadium is not just about football — it is about pride, identity, and giving young Tasmanians a reason to stay in the state instead of moving away.

Wider economic benefits

The stadium is only part of a much bigger plan for the area, which will also include new homes, shops, and businesses. Supporters say this will create jobs during construction and bring more visitors and money into Hobart in the future, through football matches, cricket games, and concerts.

The cost is under control, backers say

The government has promised that Tasmanian taxpayers will not have to pay more than $875 million — the rest will come from the AFL and the federal government. Some politicians who voted yes, even though they were unsure, said the project was about "hope" and building something for future generations.

4. Arguments Against the Stadium
Arguments in opposition

The price keeps rising

The price has grown several times: from about $715 million, to $945 million, and now $1.13 billion. Critics believe the final cost will be even higher. In August 2026, the government refused to release an updated cost estimate, which critics said showed a lack of honesty about the real price.

Experts recommended against it

The Tasmanian Planning Commission, an independent expert group, studied the project carefully and said it should not be built. It said the stadium was "too big for the site" and that its problems were bigger than its benefits. The government chose to continue anyway, because this recommendation was not legally binding.

Money could be spent elsewhere

Opponents argue that Tasmania has more urgent problems, such as underfunded hospitals and a shortage of housing. They also point out that the $240 million from the federal government is meant for housing and other improvements in the area — not for the stadium itself — which leaves a gap in the stadium's own budget.

Cultural and historical concerns

The RSL (an organisation for military veterans) is worried that the stadium is too close to Hobart's war memorial, the cenotaph, and could damage its peaceful atmosphere. Separately, the Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre has also spoken against the stadium being built at this location, which has cultural importance.

Strong public opposition

Thousands of people joined a protest against the stadium in November 2025. Critics have also raised concerns about the building process itself, including a report warning that Tasmania may not have enough concrete material for such a large construction project.

5. What Is Happening Now (late August 2026)
Where things stand
The stadium has now been officially approved and building work has started. But the argument has not fully ended — people now argue more about whether the cost and building process can be trusted, rather than whether it should be built at all.
  • Political status: Fully approved. Both parts of the Tasmanian parliament voted yes in December 2025. Tasmanian taxpayers cannot be asked to pay more than $875 million.
  • Building work: Early construction work started in July 2026. Two companies are competing for the main building contract, and one of them has been criticised in the media because of links to a company facing criminal charges — though the developer says this is not a serious problem.
  • Cost: The official public cost is still $1.13 billion, but this number has not been updated since September 2025, even though the real cost may already be higher. In August 2026, parliament rejected a request to make the government share newer cost information.
  • Timeline: The Tasmania Devils will join the AFL in 2028, but the stadium will probably not be ready until late 2030. This means the team will play its first seasons at other grounds, such as Bellerive Oval, before moving into the new stadium.
  • Wider area planning: A master plan for the whole Macquarie Point area — including new housing, shops, and a space recognising Aboriginal culture — has been released and is going through an official planning process, with public feedback expected later in 2026.
  • Ongoing opposition: Groups such as the Greens political party and RSL Tasmania continue to ask for more transparency and openly criticise the project, although it is now very unlikely to be stopped through parliament.

6. Questions to Discuss

For classroom discussion

  1. Do you think it is fair for a sports organisation like the AFL to require a government to build an expensive stadium?
  2. How should a government balance "hard to measure" benefits, like community pride, against clear financial costs?
  3. What should happen when independent experts recommend against a project, but politicians decide to continue anyway?
  4. Who should be responsible if the final cost is much higher than $1.13 billion?
  5. How important is it to protect historical or cultural places, like war memorials or Aboriginal heritage sites, when planning new buildings?

Useful vocabulary

Stadium
a large building with seats where people watch sports events
Parliament
the group of politicians who make laws for a country or state
Taxpayer
a person who pays money to the government (tax)
Cost blowout
when something ends up costing much more money than first planned
Independent commission
a group of experts who give advice without being controlled by the government
Precinct
a specific area of a city planned for a particular use
Cenotaph
a monument that honours soldiers who died in war
